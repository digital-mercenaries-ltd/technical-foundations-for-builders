# Personas for TFB and derived products

Status: working product hypotheses - 2026-08-05

These personas describe distinct situations in which someone may need **Technical Foundations for Builders** (TFB), a future **Technical Leadership for Builders** (TLB) companion, **Software Commissioning**, the **Delivery Risk Cube** (DRC), or a derived navigation product. They are not learner tracks, market segments, competence assessments or promises that every reader needs the same material.

The initial product remains the TFB Markdown guide. A future product should use these personas to test whether it improves discovery, explanation or decision-making without replacing canonical entries with personalised generated content. The relevant boundaries remain in [PROJECT_FAMILY.md](../PROJECT_FAMILY.md) and [adaptive projections](adaptive-projections.md).

## Persona summary

| Persona | Situation and immediate job | Primary fit | Derived-product implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maya, the domain founder | Has a real problem and a rapidly built product; needs to see risks before making a customer commitment. | TFB, then DRC | A project-aware risk-and-concept route. |
| Ben, the capable uneven builder | Ships reliably in a familiar stack but lacks breadth outside it. | TFB | A self-directed map with confidence-based routes. |
| Aisha, the sole technical owner | Owns software and the wider technical function of a small organisation. | TFB and future TLB | Connected system and organisation routes with explicit boundaries. |
| Tom, the product operator turned builder | Owns a workflow and increasingly changes software with AI assistance. | TFB | Contextual explanations tied to product, data and user consequences. |
| Grace, the commissioning buyer | Must choose and establish a consequential software service or supplier relationship. | Software Commissioning, with TFB/TLB/DRC links as needed | An evidence-led commissioning route and decision record. |
| Elena, the inherited-system steward | Must change a useful but poorly understood production system. | TFB, future TLB, DRC | A change-safety route driven by uncertainty and evidence. |
| Ravi, the advanced AI-assisted practitioner | Can produce and integrate code quickly; needs independent evidence and durable judgement. | TFB, DRC | Verification-led routes and transparent explanations of why a concept was selected. |

## Maya — domain founder building an initial product

Maya understands a costly industry problem, speaks regularly to prospective users and has assembled a web product with AI coding tools and a managed platform. The product works in demonstrations. She is preparing to invite real customers and may soon handle personal, commercial or operationally consequential data.

Her job is not to become an engineer before continuing. It is to recognise which assumptions have become production risks, ask better questions of AI tools and specialists, and distinguish a convincing workflow from evidence that the system is safe enough for its next commitment.

Her misleading intuition is that working screens, a successful test payment and a hosted database add up to readiness. TFB should help her recognise concepts such as data ownership, authentication versus authorisation, backups, error handling, observability and provider coupling. DRC should later help her decide what evidence matters before a private pilot, rather than treating every quality concern as equally urgent.

A derived product could ask about her users, data, integrations and next commitment, then show a small, inspectable route with the risk signal behind each recommendation. It must not infer that she lacks technical ability or certify the product from a conversational intake.

## Ben — capable builder with uneven breadth

Ben is a self-taught developer or solopreneur who has shipped several applications. He can work effectively in one framework, use source control and diagnose familiar failures, but has largely learned by solving immediate problems. He suspects gaps in areas such as networking, database behaviour, distributed failure, security, operations or accessibility.

His job is to build a map around what he already knows without being sent back through an academic curriculum. He needs concise explanations that make unknown terms recognisable, links that reveal dependencies, and enough warning signs to know when a change deserves research or expert review.

His misleading intuition is that a broad reading list or role-based roadmap will reveal the important gaps. TFB's value is editorial selection and explanation, not a longer catalogue. A future navigation layer could let Ben state a stack, concern and confidence level, then recommend a route across canonical entries while preserving the option to browse laterally. It should not convert his self-report into a skills profile or claim professional competence.

## Aisha — sole technical owner

Aisha is the only technical employee in a small company, or the technical co-founder responsible for everything that touches technology. She maintains the product while also selecting vendors, handling identity and devices, responding to customers' assurance questions, planning spend and managing key-person risk.

Her job is to keep the software system trustworthy while making the organisation's technical responsibilities visible, owned and fundable. She needs to know which questions concern the system itself and which require organisational decisions, policy, procurement or continuity planning.

TFB should support her system-level judgement without absorbing the entire accidental-chief-technology-officer role. Future TLB should cover the organisational capability she carries. A derived product should make the hand-off explicit: for example, link an access-control explanation in TFB to the future organisational responsibility for joiner-mover-leaver processes, rather than duplicating either explanation.

## Tom — product operator turned builder

Tom owns a customer-support, operations or revenue workflow. He knows the exceptions, the workarounds and the cost of a delay better than the development team did. He now changes internal tooling, prototypes automations and may connect services with AI assistance.

His job is to turn domain knowledge into useful software without silently encoding a brittle process, exposing customer data or creating an unmaintainable shadow system. He needs language for user journeys, domain rules, state changes, data quality, access boundaries, integration contracts and observable outcomes.

His misleading intuition is that the main technical risk is whether he can get the automation to run. TFB should connect technical mechanisms to the operational consequence he already understands. A future product could present concept routes through a concrete workflow or scenario, but it should not become a task tutorial or treat every business-process issue as a software problem. Some questions belong in TLB, particularly ownership, process governance and service selection.

## Grace — commissioning buyer

Grace is responsible for getting a needed capability into service. She may be choosing a software-as-a-service product, engaging a specialist supplier or commissioning an integration for a team that cannot afford a failed dependency. A promising demonstration exists, but her decision will create costs, data flows, administrative responsibilities and a future exit problem.

Her job is to establish whether the proposed service performs the real workflow and whether the organisation can operate, control and replace it. She needs an investigation proportionate to the consequence: representative trial evidence, clear account and access ownership, usable export and exit, realistic entitlement and cost, and explicit residual risks.

Her misleading intuition is that a feature list, marketplace connector or vendor assertion that an application programming interface exists proves integration fit. **Software Commissioning** is the canonical home for buyer-side evidence and the resulting service relationship. TFB explains the underlying mechanisms, such as interfaces, permissions, data, failure and recovery. TLB addresses portfolio, supplier governance and organisational decision rights. DRC can frame whether the evidence is adequate for the next commitment, but it does not choose the supplier.

## Elena — inherited-system steward

Elena has become responsible for an established application after a founder, contractor or previous team left. Customers rely on it, but its documentation is thin, changes are frightening and nobody is certain which integrations, data flows or manual workarounds remain critical.

Her job is to make the next change safer while gradually replacing hidden knowledge with inspectable evidence. She needs concepts that explain why a rewrite is not automatically safer, how interfaces and data migrations create risk, why observability and backups matter before change, and how to limit failure reach and recover when a release goes wrong.

TFB provides the mechanisms. DRC can frame the immediate question: whether the evidence shape matches a maintenance change, migration or customer commitment. Future TLB may address continuity, sourcing and decision rights. A derived product for Elena should begin with uncertainty and the next commitment, then recommend a bounded route; it should not recommend an automatic rewrite or claim that repository inspection proves system understanding.

## Ravi — advanced AI-assisted practitioner

Ravi already understands enough engineering to use coding agents productively. He can generate a prototype, integrate APIs and deploy a service quickly. His material risk is not lack of output: it is accepting generated code, infrastructure or policy language that nobody has independently understood, tested, secured or prepared to operate.

His job is to retain responsible human judgement as speed rises. He needs concise, technically serious explanations of where agentic methods change the trust boundary: permissions, credentials, generated artefact ownership, evaluation, prompt injection, dependency coupling, review and recovery.

His misleading intuition is that a strong model, a passing test suite or a polished agent workflow establishes correctness. TFB should repeatedly reconnect AI-assisted work to the conventional mechanisms that govern production outcomes. DRC should help him select evidence proportionate to the next commitment. A derived product could use transparent rules to suggest verification-oriented concepts, but must explain the recommendation and keep the user in control of what is inspected, recorded or deferred.

## Product design rules implied by the personas

1. Keep the first product useful without profiling. Every persona must be able to browse the Markdown guide without an account, quiz, repository connection or persistent progress record.
2. Route by stated situation, goal, next commitment and uncertainty—not job title alone. A founder can have deep engineering experience; a developer can be responsible for organisation-wide risk.
3. Show why a route contains each concept and allow the reader to defer, remove or reorder it. A recommendation is guidance, not a verdict about competence.
4. Keep TFB, TLB, Software Commissioning and DRC separate. TFB explains software mechanisms; TLB addresses organisational technical capability; Software Commissioning addresses a specific buyer-side service decision and relationship; and DRC frames the evidence imbalance before the next commitment.
5. Treat user input, quiz responses and authorised repository inspection as fallible signals. Minimise collected data, make it visible and correctable, and never turn it into a credential.

## Validation questions

These are hypotheses to test with representative readers before they shape a new product:

- Can a reader identify a genuinely unknown risk earlier after using a short TFB route?
- Does the explanation of why a concept was recommended increase trust and help the reader act?
- Can the reader distinguish a system-level technical question from an organisational leadership or commitment-evidence question?
- Can a commissioning buyer distinguish proof that a service performs the real workflow from a vendor's claim that it is supported?
- Does a project-aware route improve discovery without creating pressure to disclose a repository, data or personal profile?
- For an AI-assisted builder, does the route lead to independent evidence rather than greater confidence in unverified output?
